# From 0.96136 to 0.96243+: Unlocking Ensemble Lift Through Noise-Aware GBDTs and Asymmetric Feature Allocation

## Baseline Architecture and Methodological Imperatives

The primary objective of this research is to advance the predictive performance of a Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) ensemble for the Kaggle Playground Series Season 6 Episode 10 (S6E10) competition from a current Out-of-Fold (OOF) ROC-AUC of 0.96136 to an aspirational target of 0.96243 or higher [[33](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e10/data), [39](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e10/models)]. This represents a marginal but highly significant improvement of approximately 107 basis points (bps), achievable only through a disciplined, evidence-driven investigation of novel feature engineering and ensemble diversification strategies within strict constraints. The foundation of this effort is the existing production pipeline hosted at `github.com/tuboa3/airline-satisfaction` (commit 72cf301), which provides a sophisticated starting point but also highlights the challenges inherent in operating near the performance ceiling of tabular competitions [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The baseline performance is derived from a four-model ensemble, with individual OOF AUC scores of 0.96110 for LightGBM, 0.96090 for XGBoost, 0.96088 for LightGBM Extra-Trees, and 0.96072 for CatBoost [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. These results demonstrate that standard feature engineering has been extensively exploited, and further gains will necessitate a deeper understanding of the data's idiosyncrasies, including suspected label corruption and structural differences between the synthetic and original datasets.

A critical constraint is the GBDT-only policy, which explicitly permits the use of CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, and Extra Trees as both base learners and meta-learners [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. All other model families, including neural networks (e.g., TabPFN, RealMLP), linear models, SVMs, and transformers, are strictly forbidden [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This constraint fundamentally shapes the research direction, ruling out many high-performing techniques employed by top competitors who have used large stacks incorporating non-permitted models [[56](https://www.kaggle.com/code/sai10py/airline-satisfaction-qlaya), [57](https://www.kaggle.com/code?types=competitions)]. Consequently, the focus must remain squarely on optimizing the permitted GBDT family. The existing pipeline incorporates a wide array of engineered features, including indicators for physical delays and CTGAN artifacts, specialized handling for survey N/A values, interactions designed to capture Simpson's Paradox, trunk corridor features derived from the nearest FAA modes, and sophisticated route profile aggregations such as group means (`fdm_*`) and residuals (`res_*`) [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Furthermore, it leverages auxiliary expected rating priors generated from 13 multiclass classifiers trained exclusively on the original 129,880 rows of data, along with 39 interaction features derived from passenger class, travel type, and customer type [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This complex feature set underscores the need for a nuanced approach to experimentation, where new features are validated not in isolation but within the context of the entire system.

Perhaps the most distinctive aspect of the current approach is the asymmetric feature allocation strategy, which tailors feature sets to the perceived strengths of each model family [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. For instance, CatBoost receives the 39 categorical rating crosses and external logit priors, while being explicitly deprived of dense continuous route residuals that are given to LightGBM [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This strategy is based on known architectural biases: CatBoost's superior handling of categorical features makes it well-suited for the rating crosses, while LightGBM's leaf-wise tree growth is thought to better exploit dense numerical signals like `fdm_*` and `res_*` [[74](https://www.xoriant.com/blog/gradient-boosting-in-machine-learning-xgboost-lightgbm-random-forest-explained), [77](https://catboost.ai/news/catboost-enables-fast-gradient-boosting-on-decision-trees-using-gpus)]. However, this approach has produced exceptionally consequential and potentially problematic effects. Preliminary findings indicate that the 39 rating crosses improve CatBoost's performance by approximately 0.0015 ROC-AUC but harm LightGBM's by a substantial 0.0048 [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Such a large negative impact suggests that either the features are too sparse for LightGBM's splitting mechanism or they introduce an interaction pattern that the model handles poorly, highlighting the delicate balance required in asymmetric allocation. The goal of any future allocation strategy must be to foster genuine diversity—complementary error patterns that lead to a net gain in ensemble performance—rather than simply decorrelating predictions [[38](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-the-importance-of-diversity-in-ensemble-learning-34fb58fd2ed0/)].

To manage these complexities and ensure the validity of all experiments, a rigorous methodological framework is non-negotiable. The directive mandates a two-tiered validation protocol to distinguish genuine improvements from random fold variance [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The first tier, a Structural Feature Gate, requires any standalone feature block to demonstrate a minimum gain of +0.00030 (30 bps) on a GBDT's 5-fold CV score, with at least four out of five folds showing positive gains [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This threshold is justified by the approximate standard error of a single fold on ~700k rows being around 0.00007, making smaller gains statistically insignificant [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The second tier, an Ensemble Orthogonality Gate, targets a more subtle but crucial outcome: a marginal ensemble lift of +0.00010 (10 bps) IF AND ONLY IF it is accompanied by a decrease in inter-model correlation (Δr ≤ -0.010) [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This reflects the observation that the current base models exhibit extremely high pairwise Pearson correlations (0.9983–0.9991), which collapses the effective size of the ensemble and nullifies the theoretical benefit of blending [[38](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-the-importance-of-diversity-in-ensemble-learning-34fb58fd2ed0/)]. Lowering the correlation below a target of 0.985 is therefore a functional requirement to unlock the variance reduction needed to surpass the 0.96243+ threshold. Every experiment must be pre-registered with a clear hypothesis, control, treatment, metric, and acceptance/rejection criteria to prevent post-hoc rationalization of results [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This disciplined approach ensures that resources are focused on experiments with the highest potential for meaningful, reproducible impact.

This foundational understanding establishes the stage for targeted research. The path forward is not about incremental feature addition but about strategically addressing three core challenges identified in the user's directive: investigating and exploiting suspected label noise, expanding the utility of the original dataset beyond naive concatenation, and systematically engineering model diversity. Each of these areas requires a deep dive into both the data's unique characteristics and the underlying mechanics of the permitted GBDT algorithms.

## Investigating Probabilistic Label Noise and Passenger Coherence

The suspicion of approximately 3.8% label corruption in the synthetic training data is the most significant uncertainty driving the research agenda, reframing the problem from pure prediction to learning under controlled uncertainty [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. If true, this probabilistic Bernoulli noise directly impacts model training, as GBDTs, despite some inherent robustness, can adapt to mislabeled examples, especially during later boosting iterations [[2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2409.08647), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08647v2)]. Therefore, the primary objective is not to naively remove or relabel data but to develop diagnostic features that allow the model to learn to handle label ambiguity. This requires a multi-pronged approach combining forensic analysis, cross-fitted disagreement metrics, and robust training strategies, all while adhering to the strict no-circular-reasoning rule.

The first step is to determine if the 3.8% figure is a reliable estimate or a misleading artifact. It is critical to understand what quantities are actually identifiable from the synthetic training set alone versus what requires additional assumptions or a trustworthy reference population like the original dataset [[79](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=ZPa2SyGcbwh)]. A confident disagreement between a model's prediction and a label does not definitively prove the label is wrong; it could simply reflect the model's poor fit or irreducible ambiguity in the data [[79](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=ZPa2SyGcbwh)]. To circumvent this, we must construct diagnostic features that measure the _degree_ of inconsistency rather than claiming to identify ground truth. One powerful approach is to train an independent GBDT model to estimate the probability of satisfaction, $\widehat p_i = \widehat P(Y_i=1\mid X_i)$, using a cross-fitting procedure where the model is trained on all outer folds and its predictions are used to generate features for the held-out inner fold [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. From this model's output, several valuable features can be derived. The cross-fitted disagreement score, defined as $D_i=-[Y_i\log(\widehat p_i+\epsilon)+(1-Y_i)\log(1-\widehat p_i+\epsilon)]$, measures the negative log-loss, with more negative values indicating greater confidence in an incorrect prediction [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Another useful feature is the signed residual, $e_i=Y_i-\widehat p_i$, which captures the raw difference between the actual and predicted satisfaction [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Finally, a margin-adjusted disagreement score, $M_i=(2Y_i-1)(2\widehat p_i-1)$, can be used to quantify the alignment between the true label and the predicted probability [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. These features do not assert that the label is wrong; instead, they provide a signal that a particular instance is a "difficult" or "inconsistent" example for the model to predict, which is itself a valuable piece of information.

Beyond model-based disagreement, several other noise detection metrics adapted for GBDTs can be explored. Research has shown that noisy instances often exhibit larger gradient magnitudes during the boosting process because they represent a larger loss that needs to be corrected [[2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2409.08647), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08647v2)]. By tracking the absolute gradients of each sample across boosting iterations, one can identify samples with persistently large gradients as potential noise candidates. Another robust method is the Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT), which evaluates the ratio of the classifier's confidence in the assigned label versus its highest competing prediction [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08647v1), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08647v2)]. An LRT score significantly less than 1 suggests the model is uncertain about the assigned label, flagging it for review. Training dynamics can also be informative; by monitoring metrics like Confidence (average predicted probability for the true label), Variability (fluctuation in true class probabilities), and Correctness (percentage of correct classifications) over epochs, one can classify instances as "easy-to-learn" or "hard-to-learn," with the latter often corresponding to noisy labels [[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08647v2)]. The effectiveness of these methods degrades as the noise rate increases, but even partial discrimination can be beneficial [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08647v1)]. The proposed experiments should compare the standalone performance of these diagnostic features (feature-only coherence) against cross-fitted disagreement features and their combination, validating them against the Tier 1 structural gain threshold before deployment.

Once diagnostic features are created, they can be integrated into the training process through several robust strategies, avoiding the pitfalls of simple removal or relabeling. Sample weighting is a powerful technique where suspected noisy examples are down-weighted. A particularly effective approach is Clipped Cross-Entropy (CCE), which caps the per-sample loss value at a predefined threshold rather than excluding the sample entirely [[15](https://blog.allegro.tech/2023/04/learning-from-noisy-data.html)]. This mitigates the influence of high-loss examples without discarding potentially valuable data, proving highly effective in experiments with up to 20% synthetic label noise [[15](https://blog.allegro.tech/2023/04/learning-from-noisy-data.html)]. Another fundamental defense mechanism is early stopping, as GBDTs tend to learn the true labels first and only gradually adapt to noisy ones [[2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2409.08647)]. Identifying the optimal stopping point before the model overfits to noise is critical. A third, and perhaps safest, strategy is to treat the noise indicators themselves as predictive features [[78](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-025-06860-8)]. Instead of attempting to fix the data, this approach allows the final GBDT to learn how to interpret the noise flags and adjust its predictions accordingly. This aligns with the principles of Soft Label Learning, where uncertainty is explicitly modeled rather than ignored [[78](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-025-06860-8)]. For any of these strategies, it is crucial to implement them in a leakage-safe manner, using cross-fitting to ensure that the noise score for a given row is never calculated using that row's own label.

The origin of the synthetic data, a Conditional Tabular GAN (CTGAN), adds another layer of complexity [[31](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN), [46](https://medium.com/@kdk199604/ctgan-how-gans-finally-learned-to-handle-tabular-data-ebbfb169c5d8)]. CTGANs are designed to learn the statistical properties of tabular data, including distributions and correlations, but they are not perfect [[32](https://sustainabilitymethods.org/index.php/How_To_Create_Synthetic_Data_with_CTGAN)]. They can struggle to capture nuanced variable correlations and may produce data with structural imperfections that contribute to the observed distribution shift [[32](https://sustainabilitymethods.org/index.php/How_To_Create_Synthetic_Data_with_CTGAN), [68](https://www.kdnuggets.com/2023/04/unveiling-potential-ctgan-harnessing-generative-ai-synthetic-data.html)]. Studies have shown that increasing label noise levels in synthetic data degrade its quality and utility for downstream tasks, though it does not necessarily prevent the synthesizer from converging [[3](https://digitalcommons.montclair.edu/computing-facpubs/40/), [71](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-024-06629-5)]. The reported 3.8% noise could thus be a composite of genuine label flips and these structural artifacts. When using the original dataset as a reference, it is vital to verify whether its labels and row identities are valid for inference. Simply matching synthetic records to original ones based on similar feature values is a flawed assumption that can introduce its own form of leakage [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. A more principled approach would be to use the original data solely as a source for building auxiliary models (as is currently done) or for training a separate detector to distinguish real from synthetic data, providing a holistic view of the distributional mismatch [[73](https://cleanlab.ai/blog/learn/studio-synthetic-data/)]. ## Expanding Original Data Priors Beyond Conditional Expectations

The current pipeline's reliance on auxiliary expected rating priors, generated by 13 multiclass LightGBM classifiers trained exclusively on the original dataset, represents a powerful and validated strategy for leveraging external data [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. These models estimate the conditional expectation, $\widehat\mu_j = \mathbb{E}[r_j\mid X_{-j}]$, effectively creating a baseline prediction for each service rating based on all other available features . The innovation lies in expanding this concept from a single-point estimate (the mean) to a rich, probabilistic representation of the entire conditional distribution, $P(r_j=k\mid X_{-j})$. This expansion promises to capture nuances of passenger behavior that are lost in a simple mean calculation, providing the main GBDT with a much deeper understanding of passenger coherence and surprise. This approach directly addresses the label noise hypothesis by quantifying how unusual or unexpected a passenger's rating is, given their journey context.

The first and most straightforward extension is to replace or supplement the single expected rating value with the full vector of predicted class probabilities from the auxiliary models. For each of the 13 service ratings, the model outputs a probability distribution over the possible rating values (e.g., 0 to 5). Several powerful features can be derived from this distribution. The maximum predicted probability, $\max_k P(r_j=k\mid X_{-j})$, serves as a natural confidence score for the most likely rating, with low values indicating high uncertainty or ambiguity in the passenger's experience [[14](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/text2vec/html/coherence.html)]. A related feature is the conditional entropy, $H(r_j\mid X_{-j}) = -\sum_k P(r_j=k\mid X_{-j}) \log P(r_j=k\mid X_{-j})$, which quantifies the overall uncertainty of the rating given the predictors [[12](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-topic-coherence-measures-4aa41339634c/)]. High entropy regions correspond to contexts where passenger satisfaction is unpredictable, signaling areas where the main model may require more guidance. Perhaps the most insightful feature is the conditional likelihood of the observed rating, $P(r_j=r_{\text{obs}}\mid X_{-j})$. This directly answers the question: "Given this passenger's attributes, how probable was it that they would give this specific rating?" A very low probability indicates a surprising or anomalous response, which could be a sign of either a genuine outlier or a potential label error.

Going even further, the negative log-likelihood, $L_j = -\log P(r_j\mid X_{-j})$, provides a robust "surprise score" analogous to topic coherence metrics in NLP [[13](https://medium.com/data-science/c%E1%B5%A5-topic-coherence-explained-fc70e2a85227)]. Just as a topic coherence metric evaluates how well a set of words co-occur based on a reference corpus, this feature evaluates how well a passenger's rating co-occurs with their other observed attributes [[12](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-topic-coherence-measures-4aa41339634c/)]. A high surprise score signifies a rare or inconsistent passenger profile, which is a strong signal for the main model to pay closer attention to. This directly links back to the label noise investigation, as instances with both low satisfaction and high rating surprise may warrant special consideration. Critically, all these features must be generated in a leakage-safe manner. Since they are derived from the original dataset, their predictions must be calibrated or adjusted to account for the distributional differences between the original and synthetic data. A naive application of the original-data-trained models to synthetic rows is likely to produce biased predictions due to covariate shift [[71](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-024-06629-5)]. A safer approach is to create separate models for each data source (original and synthetic) and feed their predictions as distinct features, allowing the final GBDT to learn when to trust which prior. This respects the domain shift and avoids invalid distribution alignment.

The next logical step is to move beyond modeling each rating independently and investigate richer, joint dependencies between different service dimensions. The current methodology treats the 13 ratings as conditionally independent given the other features, which is a simplifying assumption [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. In reality, passenger satisfaction is a holistic experience; a poor inflight entertainment rating might be highly predictive of a poor seat comfort rating, especially within certain classes or travel contexts. To capture this, we can train auxiliary models to predict the conditional distribution of one rating given another, $P(r_a\mid r_b, C)$, where $C$ contains legitimate contextual variables like passenger class, travel type, and customer type [[21](https://github.com/navadkarsujit/Airline-Passenger-Satisfaction-ML-Project)]. Examples of such relationships include predicting inflight entertainment conditioned on seat comfort and Class, or online boarding conditioned on check-in service and customer type. These can be implemented using a multiclass GBDT where the feature space includes the conditioning variables $r_b$ and $C$. The resulting predicted distribution $P(r_a\mid r_b, C)$ can then be summarized using the same suite of features as before: maximum probability, entropy, and negative log-likelihood. This approach creates a network of conditional priors that encodes a more structured understanding of passenger psychology. However, this introduces a combinatorial explosion in the number of models required. A principled feature-selection or block-ablation strategy is essential to manage the feature count and avoid introducing excessive noise. One could start by focusing on a few hypothesized strong dependencies, such as those between adjacent stages of the travel journey (e.g., check-in -> boarding, inflight service -> baggage handling).

Finally, the choice of training data for these auxiliary models is paramount. Should they be trained on the original data only, the synthetic data only, or a combination? Naive concatenation of the datasets is known to degrade performance, reducing the CV score by approximately 0.0038 due to significant distribution shifts [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The Total Variation Distance (TVD) between the flight distance distributions is approximately 0.21, and for age, it is 0.15, confirming this shift [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Therefore, training a single model on a combined dataset would force it to learn a compromised representation that is suboptimal for both domains. The recommended approach is to maintain separate models for each data source and treat their predictions as distinct features. This allows the final GBDT to act as a meta-learner, determining the appropriate weight to give to the original-data prior versus the synthetic-data prior depending on the input features of each test instance. This method respects the underlying domain shift and has been shown to be a robust way to combine information from disparate sources [[69](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.10424v1)]. For instance, for a passenger with extreme age or distance values that are rare in the original data, the model would learn to rely more heavily on priors learned from the broader distribution of the synthetic data. This careful, modular approach maximizes the utility of the original dataset without falling into the trap of naive augmentation.

| **Conditional Entropy**            | $H(r_j\mid X_{-j}) = -\sum_k \widehat P(r_j=k\mid X_{-j}) \log \widehat P(r_j=k\mid X_{-j})$ | Quantifies the uncertainty of the rating given the predictors. High entropy indicates unpredictability.               | Derived from the conditional probability vector. Requires smoothing to avoid issues with zero probabilities.                            |
| **Negative Log-Likelihood**        | $L_j = -\log \widehat P(r_j=r_{\text{obs}}\mid X_{-j})$                                      | Measures the "surprise" or rarity of the observed rating given the context. High values indicate anomalous responses. | Derived from the conditional probability vector.                                                                                        |
| **Joint Conditional Probability**  | $\widehat P(r_a=k\mid r_b=c, C)$                                                             | Predicts the distribution of rating $r_a$ given a specific value $c$ of rating $r_b$ and contextual variables $C$.    | Train auxiliary models with a subset of features including $r_b$ and $C$. Summarize output with MaxProb, Entropy, etc.                  |
| **Source-Specific Priors**         | Separate models for Original vs. Synthetic data.                                             | Two sets of predictions for the same feature (e.g., `orig_mu_wifi`, `synth_mu_wifi`).                                 | Prevents domain shift by forcing the final model to learn a fusion strategy.                                                            |

By systematically expanding the auxiliary priors in this manner, the model gains access to a much richer set of signals derived from the original dataset. These features are not just another set of inputs; they are a structured, probabilistic encoding of passenger behavior that can help the GBDT disentangle true signals from noise and navigate the complex landscape of passenger satisfaction.

## Uncovering Latent Route Structure in Flight Distance

The hypothesis that the continuous `Flight Distance` variable contains latent information beyond simple travel length is a compelling avenue for novel signal extraction, especially given the absence of explicit origin and destination airport identifiers [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The existing feature engineering already acknowledges this to some extent through the creation of trunk corridor features (e.g., nearest distance to top 10 FAA modes) and fine-grained route group means (`fdm_*`) and residuals (`res_*`) [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. However, these approaches can be significantly expanded and refined by treating `Flight Distance` as a rich signal to be decomposed, rather than a simple numeric feature to be discretized. This research track focuses on developing a suite of representations that can expose hidden periodicities, cluster structures, and local patterns that GBDTs can exploit to improve satisfaction prediction. The key is to validate these features rigorously against the Tier 1 structural gain threshold to ensure they provide genuine, non-trivial information.

A foundational step is to move beyond treating `Flight Distance` as a raw float and instead create representations based on its frequency distribution and local neighborhood. The first enhancement is to add features that capture the global and local rarity of each distance value. By creating a frequency map of all distance values in the training data, one can generate a `distance_rarity` feature for each row, representing how infrequent its specific distance is. Very frequent distances are strong candidates for major trunk routes, and their high frequency can be a powerful signal in itself. This can be complemented by smoothed local statistics. Instead of looking at a single distance value, one can calculate target statistics (e.g., mean satisfaction) within small, symmetric or adaptive neighborhoods around each value . For example, for a given distance `d`, one could calculate the mean satisfaction within the range `[d-w, d+w]`. Using a cross-fitting scheme, the encoder for a row is trained on all other rows, preventing label leakage [[59](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/preprocessing/plot_target_encoder_cross_val.html)]. Varying the width `w` of the neighborhood can capture different scales of structure, from very local trends (e.g., ±50 miles) to broader regional clusters [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Density-based approaches, such as calculating the number of other flights within a certain distance window, can also highlight hubs of activity that may correlate with different types of passenger experiences.

Building on this, mathematical transformations offer a powerful way to uncover periodic structures that may correspond to common flight segments or airline hub-and-spoke patterns. Harmonic decomposition, specifically using modulo operations, can reveal cyclic patterns. For a candidate modulus `m`, one can create features `d % m` (the remainder) and `floor(d/m)` (the quotient) . These features can highlight recurring patterns in flight distances that are multiples of `m`. For instance, a common domestic route segment might be 500 miles, and a modulus of 500 would make all such segments resolve to a value near zero. The challenge with this approach is selecting an appropriate modulus `m` without using validation labels, which would constitute severe leakage [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. A principled, leakage-safe method is to analyze the empirical distribution of `Flight Distance` on the training data alone. Candidate moduli could be selected as divisors of common distance values, or one could apply clustering algorithms to the distance values themselves to identify natural groupings that suggest a repeating structure. An alternative to the discontinuous modulo operation is to use smooth periodic representations like `sin(2πd/m)` and `cos(2πd/m)` . These functions encode cyclical information more gracefully, avoiding the artificial jump at multiples of `m` and potentially being easier for tree-based models to learn. However, this introduces a multiple-testing problem, as the optimal `m` is unknown. This can be managed by testing a principled search space of candidate moduli and applying a stricter significance threshold.

Another powerful approach is to perform direct clustering on the `Flight Distance` variable, augmented with other relevant contextual variables. Since explicit airports are missing, this clustering effectively learns discrete route-proxy categories from the available data. One could use a density-based algorithm like DBSCAN, which can find arbitrarily shaped clusters and identify outliers, on a feature space consisting of `Flight Distance`, `Age`, `Class`, and other demographic or journey-related features . The resulting cluster IDs would serve as a new categorical feature representing an inferred route. It is crucial to evaluate the stability of these clusters across different outer folds and random seeds to ensure they represent robust, underlying structure rather than transient artifacts of a single data partition. Before clustering, it is important to scale the features appropriately. Once the clusters are formed, one can derive a host of target-encoded features from them, such as the mean satisfaction per cluster (`fdm_*` equivalent), cluster size, and distance to the nearest cluster center. This hierarchical grouping approach moves beyond simple binning and can recover more complex, non-linear relationships in the data.

Finally, all newly proposed route features must undergo a rigorous evaluation to assess their incremental value and stability. The evaluation plan should include:

1.  **Performance of Route Features Alone:** First, test the standalone predictive power of the new route features (e.g., frequency, modulo, clustering IDs) to see if they contain any signal at all.
2.  **Interaction with Contextual Variables:** Assess how the route features interact with other contextual variables. For example, does a route's effect on satisfaction depend on the passenger's `Class`?
3.  **Incremental Contribution:** Measure the incremental AUC gain of adding the new route features on top of the existing `fdm_*` and `res_*` blocks. This determines if they are capturing novel information.
4.  **Stability Analysis:** Report performance across outer folds, random seeds, and separately on the synthetic versus original populations to gauge robustness. Features that perform well on one fold but collapse on others are likely fragile artifacts.
5.  **Comparison of Representations:** Directly compare the performance of different route representations (e.g., exact-distance categoricals vs. harmonic features vs. clustering IDs) to identify the most effective approach.

The following table outlines a planned experimental sequence for exploring these representations, detailing the feature formulas and computational considerations.

| Experiment ID | Feature Representation        | Formula / Description                                            | Recommended Search Space                                                            | Complexity & Risk                                                                               |
| :------------ | :---------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| R-1           | Distance Frequency/Rarity     | `1 / (1 + frequency(distance))` or `rank(distance) / total_rows` | Not Applicable                                                                      | Low. Fast vectorized operation.                                                                 |
| R-2           | Smoothed Local Statistics     | Mean satisfaction in a distance window `[d-w, d+w]`.             | `w` in {10, 25, 50, 100} miles.                                                     | Medium. By systematically pursuing this line of inquiry, it is possible to extract a wealth of structured information from the seemingly simple `Flight Distance` column. This not only creates novel features for the main model but also builds upon the existing route profile logic in a more principled and comprehensive manner, directly contributing to the goal of closing the generalization gap.

## Engineering Model-Specific Feature Allocation for Genuine Diversity

The pursuit of a higher-performing ensemble hinges on moving beyond mere correlation reduction to the deliberate cultivation of "good diversity"—a state where individual models make complementary errors, thereby improving the collective prediction [[38](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-the-importance-of-diversity-in-ensemble-learning-34fb58fd2ed0/)]. The current pipeline's asymmetric feature allocation strategy is a step in this direction, but its reported success and failure (e.g., rating crosses harming LightGBM) suggest an ad-hoc approach that warrants a more systematic and evidence-based overhaul [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The goal is to construct a feature-allocation matrix that tailors each model's input with the explicit purpose of amplifying its unique strengths and minimizing its weaknesses, leading to a blend whose performance exceeds that of its strongest constituent member. This requires a deep understanding of the inductive biases of CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, and Extra Trees.

The first step is to construct a comprehensive inventory of all validated feature blocks. This inventory serves as the raw material for the allocation matrix. Based on the provided context, this list includes:

- **Raw Features:** Basic passenger and flight demographics (Age, Gender, FamilySize).
- **Physical Delays & CTGAN Artifacts:** Flags for missing arrival delay, zero-delay indicators, and calculated discrepancy metrics.
- **Survey N/A Handling:** Binary flags for zero-ratings and adjusted rating values.
- **Simpson's Paradox Interactions:** Crosses between time convenience and travel/customer type.
- **Route Profiles:** Existing `fdm_*` (group means) and `res_*` (residuals) features.
- **Auxiliary Expected Rating Priors:** The 13 conditional expectation features from the original data.
- **Rating Crosses:** The 39 interactions between ratings and categorical variables (Class, Travel Type, Customer Type).
- **Psychometrics:** Rasch trait logits, survey entropy, and intra-passenger variance.
- **Novel Signal Blocks:** To be developed from Tracks A, B, and C (e.g., conditional rating likelihoods, harmonic distance features, joint rating priors).

With this inventory established, the core task is to build a feature-allocation matrix with rows representing feature blocks and columns representing the four permitted model families. Each cell in the matrix will be labeled **Core**, **Test**, or **Exclude**, accompanied by a rationale based on empirical evidence, model bias, and risk assessment.

The rationale for these decisions is grounded in the known architectural differences between the GBDT implementations. CatBoost is renowned for its native and efficient handling of categorical features, using ordered boosting to mitigate overfitting, making it ideal for features like the 39 rating crosses [[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03705v5), [77](https://catboost.ai/news/catboost-enables-fast-gradient-boosting-on-decision-trees-using-gpus)]. LightGBM employs a leaf-wise tree growth strategy, which can build very deep trees and is often very efficient at finding complex patterns in dense numerical data, suggesting it is a good candidate for the continuous `fdm_*` and `res_*` features [[27](https://mlbook.jyotirmoy.net/book_content/024-gradient_boosting_trees.html), [74](https://www.xoriant.com/blog/gradient-boosting-in-machine-learning-xgboost-lightgbm-random-forest-explained)]. XGBoost uses a depth-wise tree growth strategy and is known for its flexibility and strong regularization capabilities, making it a robust generalist that can benefit from a balanced mix of features [[74](https://www.xoriant.com/blog/gradient-boosting-in-machine-learning-xgboost-lightgbm-random-forest-explained)]. Extra Trees, as an ensemble of extremely randomized trees, introduces additional randomness at each split, which can help decorrelate models and reduce variance, making it a good candidate for features that might otherwise cause overfitting in a standard GBDT [[38](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-the-importance-of-diversity-in-ensemble-learning-34fb58fd2ed0/)].

Based on these principles, a provisional allocation matrix can be formulated. The initial decision to give CatBoost the rating crosses and exclude them from LightGBM is a strong hypothesis supported by their respective strengths. The dramatic reported performance degradation of LightGBM (-0.0048 AUC) when given these features is a critical data point that strongly supports an **Exclude** decision for LightGBM [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Conversely, giving CatBoost these features as **Core** is a high-priority experiment to validate. Similarly, giving LightGBM the continuous route statistics (`fdm_*`, `res_*`) as **Core** is a sound hypothesis, while giving them to CatBoost as **Test** is a lower-risk way to explore their potential benefit without risking CatBoost's performance. The existing psychometric and delay features are likely to be generally beneficial and can be designated **Core** for all models initially.

The "Test" category is reserved for features that show promise but whose impact is not yet fully understood. This includes the novel features from the other research tracks, such as conditional rating likelihoods, harmonic distance features, and joint rating priors. These should be added to the "Core" set of one or two models based on their theoretical alignment with the model's bias and then evaluated against the two-tiered validation protocol. For example, the conditional likelihood features, which measure rating surprise, might be particularly useful for CatBoost's categorical-focused learning. The harmonic distance features, which reveal periodic structures, might resonate well with LightGBM's ability to find complex patterns. This systematic, staged approach prevents the combinatorial explosion of testing every possible feature subset simultaneously and allows for a controlled, interpretable investigation of model-feature interactions.

Measuring diversity correctly is as important as allocating features. While the directive rightly warns against forcing correlation below 0.985 as an unconditional requirement, it remains a useful diagnostic tool [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The Pearson correlation of raw OOF predictions is a good starting point, but it should be supplemented with other metrics. Spearman correlation (on rankings) can detect monotonic but non-linear relationships that Pearson might miss. Examining the correlation of centered errors (residuals) is often more insightful, as it isolates the part of the prediction that the models got wrong [[38](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-the-importance-of-diversity-in-ensemble-learning-34fb58fd2ed0/)]. Pairwise AUC differences and disagreement metrics (e.g., Yule's Q) can provide a more granular view of how often models disagree and whether that disagreement is productive ("good diversity") or detrimental ("bad diversity") [[38](https://towardsdatascience.com/understanding-the-importance-of-diversity-in-ensemble-learning-34fb58fd2ed0/)]. The ultimate test of diversity is the held-out blend gain. An experiment is successful not just if it produces a standalone lift, but if it leads to a better-performing blend, ideally satisfying the Tier 2 condition of a marginal lift coupled with a significant drop in correlation.

By systematically testing the allocations and measuring the resulting diversity and blend performance, it becomes possible to move beyond the current performance ceiling and inch closer to the 0.96243+ target.

## Ensemble Optimization and Strategic Validation Protocol

The final stage of this research involves consolidating the gains from feature engineering and model-specific allocation into a cohesive, optimized ensemble. This process is not merely about combining predictions but about doing so in a way that maximizes the benefits of the carefully cultivated diversity. It requires a robust validation protocol to confirm that any observed improvements are genuine and not statistical artifacts, and a defensible selection procedure to choose the final blend. The overarching goal is to achieve the aspirational target of 0.96243+ ROC-AUC by ensuring that the final ensemble consistently outperforms its strongest individual member, a feat made possible only by successfully lowering the effective model correlation towards the Tier 2 target of below 0.985 [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)].

The ensemble construction itself offers several options, all constrained to the permitted GBDT-only policy. The simplest method is equal-weight probability averaging, where the predictions from the four base models are simply averaged [[4](https://builder.aws.com/content/3JInLz1K40OAXJvouhJpNn14fZC/building-a-gradient-boosted-ensemble-pipeline-lightgbm-xgboost-and-catboost-for-kaggle)]. While easy to implement, this assumes all models are equally reliable, which is demonstrably not the case. A more sophisticated approach is rank averaging, which averages the ranks of the predictions from each model, making the blend more robust to miscalibration [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. Logit-space averaging, where predictions are transformed via the logit function before averaging, can sometimes improve calibration and performance [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. However, the most powerful option, consistent with the GBDT-only constraint, is to use a shallow GBDT meta-learner to learn the optimal blending weights [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. This meta-learner would take the four base-model OOF predictions as input features and be trained to minimize the loss on the held-out data. Crucially, this meta-learner must itself be trained using a proper cross-fitting procedure: for each outer fold, predictions from the base models are generated using an internal cross-validation scheme, and these leak-free predictions are then used to train the meta-learner for that fold [[59](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/preprocessing/plot_target_encoder_cross_val.html)]. The final blended prediction for a given row is then obtained by feeding its four base-model predictions into the model-specific meta-learner that was trained on data from a different fold. This ensures that the blending process is truly out-of-sample and maintains the integrity of the final validation.

To select the best blend, a nested or held-out procedure is necessary. Optimizing blend weights on the same OOF predictions used for validation leads to an overly optimistic and contaminated result [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. A robust procedure would involve a two-level cross-validation loop. The outer loop defines the final test harness (e.g., 5-fold CV). Within each outer fold, the inner loop is used for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection for the base models. After the base models are finalized, their predictions across all outer folds are used as input for the meta-learner optimization. The meta-learner's hyperparameters are tuned on a separate validation set constructed from the inner folds, ensuring that the blending strategy is not overfitting to the specific noise patterns of the OOF predictions. This meticulous process guarantees that the reported final AUC is an unbiased estimate of the ensemble's true generalization performance.

Validation of the marginal gains achieved through this entire process is paramount. Given the tiny target improvement of ~107 bps, standard hypothesis testing must be approached with extreme care [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)]. The naive application of a paired Student's t-test to k-fold cross-validation results is statistically invalid because the performance estimates from different folds are not independent; they share overlapping training data [[8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-12026-2), [10](https://rasbt.github.io/mlxtend/user_guide/evaluate/paired_ttest_kfold_cv/)]. This dependence violates the core assumption of the t-test and can lead to an inflated Type I error rate, falsely declaring a significant difference where none exists [[9](https://www.machinelearningmastery.com/statistical-significance-tests-for-comparing-machine-learning-algorithms/)]. To address this, more robust statistical tests are required. DeLong's test is specifically designed for comparing the AUCs of two correlated models and is considered more robust to the dependent samples issue inherent in CV [[7](http://practicalcheminformatics.blogspot.com/2020/05/some-thoughts-on-comparing.html)]. Alternatively, the corrected resampled t-test, based on the work of Nadeau and Bengio, adjusts the variance estimate of the t-statistic to account for both variability from different training sets and different test examples, providing a more conservative and accurate inference [[9](https://www.machinelearningmastery.com/statistical-significance-tests-for-comparing-machine-learning-algorithms/)]. For any comparison, it is also crucial to report confidence intervals for the AUC difference, which can be estimated using bootstrapping. This provides a clearer picture of the uncertainty surrounding the estimate and helps distinguish a practically meaningful difference from a statistically significant but negligible one [[7](http://practicalcheminformatics.blogspot.com/2020/05/some-thoughts-on-comparing.html)]. The two-tiered validation protocol (Tier 1 and Tier 2) acts as a practical filter, ensuring that only structurally meaningful and diversity-enhancing changes are incorporated into the final ensemble [[62](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-energy-behavior-of-prosumers/writeups/sinan-sercan-37th-place-solution-feature-engineeri)].

In synthesis, the path to the 0.96243+ target is a multi-stage process of discovery, validation, and consolidation. The investigation began by establishing a rigorous baseline and a strict methodological framework. It then delved into the core challenges: diagnosing and mitigating the effects of suspected label noise, expanding the utility of the original dataset beyond simple concatenation, and extracting latent structure from the `Flight Distance` variable. Each of these avenues yielded a set of novel feature families—conditional rating priors, route proxy features, and noise consistency indicators—that were designed to provide the GBDT ensemble with a richer, more nuanced understanding of the data. The subsequent phase involved translating these insights into a strategic feature-allocation matrix, deliberately tailoring feature sets to the inductive biases of each model family to foster genuine, productive diversity. The final step is the careful construction and validation of the ensemble itself, using a robust meta-learner and a statistically sound validation protocol to ensure that the cumulative effect of these small, targeted improvements translates into a significant and reproducible lift in performance.

The most plausible path forward involves a phased implementation, beginning with the lowest-risk, highest-information experiments. The immediate priority should be to implement and validate the expanded auxiliary rating priors (Track B), as this extends a working component of the pipeline with a strong theoretical basis in coherence and noise detection. Following that, the route inference features (Track C) and the systematic validation of the asymmetric feature allocation (Track D) should be pursued. Throughout this process, every result must be scrutinized against the two-tiered validation protocol to filter out statistical noise and ensure that every change contributes meaningfully to the goal. While the 0.96243+ target is ambitious, the evidence suggests it is plausible, provided that the research proceeds with discipline, rigor, and a relentless focus on distinguishing genuine improvement from random fluctuation.
